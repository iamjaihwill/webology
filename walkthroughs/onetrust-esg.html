OneTrust ESG

Directing the discovery research and product vision for OneTrust's expansion into the ESG market

Role

Director of UX Design

Company

OneTrust

Team

2 designers, 2 product managers

Domain

ESG · Sustainability Software · Strategy

Key Outcomes

  • Directed the early-stage strategic design work for OneTrust's expansion into the ESG market — the discovery research, user journey, and high-fidelity product vision that informed MVP scoping with leadership
  • Made the strategic call to invest in deep market research before product design — secondary research on ESG frameworks, primary research with five customers across regions and program-maturity stages, and synthesis into how-might-we questions stakeholders could actually act on
  • Anchored the product vision in the customer's actual journey (assessment → goals → tracking → reporting) rather than in OneTrust's existing architecture — producing a vision that influenced what the team built rather than constrained it
  • Aligned product, design, and executive leadership around a critical-needs MVP through journey-driven prioritization — the kind of cross-functional work that distinguishes a strategic design function from a delivery one

THE BUSINESS STAKES

Entering the ESG Market with a Strategic Design Function

ESG — Environmental, Social, and Governance — was becoming a defining category for enterprise software. Investors were demanding metrics on supply-chain transparency, greenhouse-gas emissions, fair labor practices. Consumers were holding companies accountable in real time. For OneTrust, already established in privacy and governance, the question wasn't whether to enter the ESG space but how to do it with the same rigor that had built the privacy platform's market position.

A new product entry in a category like this can fail in two predictable ways: shipping too late, after competitors have defined the space, or shipping too fast, with a product designed around assumptions about customers the team never tested. The work my team owned was making sure neither happened — producing the discovery rigor and product vision that gave OneTrust leadership a credible go/no-go decision and a clear MVP scope.

KEY DECISIONS & TRADEOFFS

Research First, Mocks Second — Even Under Time Pressure

The pull on a new-product engagement is always toward visible artifacts — mocks the executive team can react to, prototypes that can be demo'd to potential customers. The pull is real because executive alignment is real. But mocks built before the customer's journey is understood produce vision documents that don't survive contact with users, and they bake in assumptions that become expensive to undo later.

I made the call to ground the engagement in research first. Two phases of discovery before any high-fidelity work: secondary research on ESG frameworks and market vocabulary, then primary interviews with five customers across regions and program-maturity stages. Only after the user journey was synthesized and the MVP scope had a defensible foundation did the team move to vision mocks. That sequencing — protecting the research investment from the pressure to skip ahead — is what made the eventual mocks land with leadership rather than start a re-litigation cycle.

APPROACH

Four Phases, Each One Earning the Next

Phase 1 — Understanding the Market. Before any user contact, the team read the ESG literature and reviewed existing standards (the certifications, frameworks, and reporting structures customers would need to support). That work both informed our interview script and meant we showed up to customer conversations sounding credible.

Phase 2 — User Discovery. Five semi-structured customer interviews across geographies and ESG program maturity. The synthesis came together as an affinity map and a set of how-might-we questions stakeholders could engage with directly — not raw transcripts, not a 60-slide research deck, but the form of artifact that travels well across functions.

Phase 3 — User Journey. The synthesis fed into a user journey that became the alignment surface between Product and UX. Requirements got written against the journey, not against the company's existing product architecture. That orientation matters: it produced a feature set scoped to the customer's actual ESG-program lifecycle.

Customer journey synthesis — three phases of an ESG manager's working cycle, each with steps, opportunities, and pain points surfaced from the discovery interviews. This artifact became the alignment surface for Product, UX, and executive leadership.

Phase 4 — Product Vision. With the requirements grounded in research, the team produced high-fidelity vision mocks targeting the four critical tasks an ESG manager faces: materiality assessment (where to focus the program), framework tracking (which standards to comply with), task management across departments (who owns what), and metrics tracking with historical trend (are we improving?). Each was anchored in something a user had told us mattered — not a feature an executive had asked for in a vacuum.

Materiality Assessment

Interactive prioritization graph — stakeholders rank initiatives by significance and ability to impact. Where the ESG program decides what to focus on first.

Framework Tracking & Recommendations

Track progress against certifications and frameworks; surface relevant news and guidance from a connected ESG knowledge base.

Task Management

Centralized task tracking across departments — addressing the dispersed-information pain point research surfaced. Each task linked back to specific initiatives, frameworks, and metrics.

Metrics & Historical Trend

Quantitative metrics tracked over time — with integration paths into the tools where the underlying data already lives.

WHAT THE WORK PRODUCED

A Strategic Foundation, Not Just a Feature Set

The deliverable was a research-grounded product vision that aligned executive leadership, gave Product a defensible MVP scope, and gave UX a journey to design against rather than a feature list to translate. The vision targeted four critical tasks — materiality assessment, framework and certification tracking, centralized task management for the cross-departmental work that ESG programs require, and metrics tracking with historical trends — each tied back to a customer signal from the discovery interviews. Subsequent design and research investment refined the proto-personas through targeted discovery and built out the rest of the platform on the foundation this engagement set.

Outcomes

Discovery research, user journey, and high-fidelity product vision delivered to OneTrust leadership · MVP scope defined against research-grounded customer needs · foundation laid for OneTrust's broader ESG product investment. Specific shipped product detail and customer adoption data is available in conversation for serious opportunities.

IMPACT & OUTCOMES

What the Foundation Made Possible

The most important outcome wasn't the visible artifact — it was the operating model the engagement set. UX entering a new-product strategy at the discovery phase, holding the line on research before mocks, producing artifacts that traveled across functions: that became the pattern OneTrust drew on for subsequent product expansions. The ESG market entry got a research-led foundation rather than a speculative one.

The trajectory from the vision deck to what shipped tracked closely to the foundational journey map the team aligned on — the executive team made the call to fund the ESG product line on the strength of the discovery work, and the framework coverage and customer-segment prioritization that came out of the research carried through into the eventual product launch. The piece I'm most proud of isn't the artifact — it's that the discovery work survived contact with the realities of engineering, sales, and partnership constraints without being reframed beyond recognition.

RETROSPECTIVE

What I'd Do Differently

Five customer interviews across regions and maturity stages was the right shape to anchor the strategy, but I'd go deeper before going wider next time. A second pass with two or three of those customers — following them into their actual workflows after the initial conversation — would have caught a handful of context-specific frictions the broader sample couldn't reveal. I'd also pull executive alignment earlier into the persona work. We landed the personas cleanly with the product team, but the executive review was a separate, later conversation; running those in parallel would have surfaced the strategic disagreements during discovery rather than after the vision deck was already drawn.
