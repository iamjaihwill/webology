OneTrust Privacy — Vendor Map

Replacing a flat vendor table with an interactive map pattern that became reusable IP for the OneTrust design system

Role

Director of UX Design

Company

OneTrust

Surface

Consent & Preferences product line

Domain

Privacy Software · Data Visualization · B2B Enterprise

Key Outcomes

  • Directed the redesign of a vendor-management surface that was failing customers — replacing a flat, hard-to-scan table with an interactive vendor map that surfaced previously buried information about the privacy-compliance chain
  • Made the strategic call to invest in visual and interaction design as core craft inside an established UX organization — rare in design teams that have already standardized on patterns, but the right move when a new visualization could materially improve a high-stakes compliance workflow
  • Established a reusable map-pattern specification that informed how the OneTrust design system handled hierarchical-relationship visualizations — a piece of design IP the rest of the org could draw from
  • Held the quality bar through specs and developer-handoff documentation with edge cases and component states defined — raising the implementation precision and shortening the engineering ramp time

THE BUSINESS STAKES

Privacy Compliance Runs on Vendor Visibility

OneTrust's Consent & Preferences product helps organizations comply with privacy laws governing how their customers' data is collected on websites and in products. The compliance picture turns on which vendors a customer's website actually loads — not just the vendors a customer thinks they have a contract with, but the chain of third-party vendors those primary vendors call indirectly. A privacy team that can't see the full chain can't make accurate compliance decisions, and a single missed vendor in that chain is the kind of thing that becomes a regulatory finding.

The existing surface was a long flat table mixing tags and vendors in one undifferentiated list. It scaled badly. Privacy teams couldn't quickly see what was new since the last scan, couldn't tell where in the chain a given vendor sat, and couldn't easily distinguish a known vendor from an unknown one. The compliance reality had moved past what the table could represent.

Three Problems with the Legacy Table

Unclear vendors. Tags surfaced by name only; no vendor detail without clicking. Unknown vendors not tracked at all.

Hard to see new changes. Privacy teams had to manually compare scans to identify new vendors — with 30+ tags per scan, this was time-consuming and error-prone.

No visibility in the chain. The Initiator Chain showed only the previous vendor calling a given tag, never the full position within the broader vendor lineage.

KEY DECISIONS & TRADEOFFS

Investing in Visualization Pattern Work in an Established UX Org

The instinct in a mature UX organization is to lean on existing patterns — a better-designed table, better filters, better sort. That instinct is usually right. It produces consistent product, scales the design team, and avoids the trap of every project reinventing its own components. But the instinct is wrong when the underlying information has a structure the existing patterns can't represent well, and that was the situation here.

I made the call to invest in a new visualization pattern: a vendor map that could show chain relationships, surface new vendors visibly, and let users expand and collapse to focus on a single vendor's lineage. That meant explicitly authorizing the team to do visual and interaction-design work that would normally not have been on the table. The trade-off was deliberate: more upfront design investment in exchange for a pattern that would solve a class of problems, not just this one screen. That's leadership work as much as design work — deciding when the system bends and when it gets extended.

APPROACH

Four Options, Two Tested, One Shipped

The team explored four visualization options at low fidelity — two radial-graph variants, two horizontal-tree variants — weighing each against the customer's actual reading task: surface new vendors, show chain position, filter on what matters.

Option 1

Radial graph — vendors arranged around a center node by relationship distance.

Option 2

Radial graph with concentric sections — vendor level encoded as circular distance from center.

Option 3

Horizontal tree graph — columns by level of vendor (primary, third-party, fourth-party).

Option 4

Horizontal tree graph — columns grouped by primary vendor, descendants nested below.

Two finalists went to testing. The radial design surfaced more vendors per screen and conveyed relationships more clearly. That became the direction. The handoff to engineering included specs for vendor-card states, edge cases, and interaction patterns — the kind of documentation that turns a design recommendation into something developers can build with confidence and quality engineers can verify.

WHAT SHIPPED

A Map, Not a Table

The shipped Vendor Map opens by surfacing exactly what a privacy team needs to see first: new vendors detected in the most recent scan, color-coded for quick recognition, with their chain position visible at a glance. Hover interactions reveal parent and child relationships. Expand-collapse controls let a user focus on a single vendor's chain or zoom out to the full picture. Information architecture leads with vendor identity, then progressively discloses tag detail — reversing the previous pattern that buried vendor context behind a sea of tag URLs.

Default map view — new vendors from the most recent scan are highlighted, with the broader vendor chain rendered as a navigable radial graph. Toggle controls in the header let users switch between “new only” and the full map.

Hover interaction — selecting a vendor highlights its full lineage in the chain, letting privacy teams trace relationships without leaving the map view.

Detail panel — vendor identity surfaced first (name, tag URL, category), with progressive disclosure into the deeper view-details flow.

The reusable pattern that came out of the project was the highest-leverage deliverable: a documented map specification with state definitions, interaction rules, and the visual language for hierarchical relationships. That pattern was available to other product lines that needed similar visualizations — data lineage, asset relationships, anywhere a tree/chain structure had to be both readable and scannable.

HANDOFF

Specs that Shorten the Engineering Ramp

For a new pattern entering the design system, the dev handoff is where leverage is won or lost. The specs the team produced documented every state of the vendor card — default, hover (main card), hover (collapse icon), chain-highlighted, selected/focus — with exact color tokens (blue300, blue50, neutralgrey0) and behavior notes for the count/expanding affordance. That documentation rigor is unusual on most projects, and it's deliberate: it shortens the engineering ramp, reduces the back-and-forth on edge cases, and makes the eventual implementation match the design intent.

Outcomes

IMPACT & OUTCOMES

What the Pattern Made Possible

The shipped Vendor Map solved the immediate problem — surfacing the privacy chain in a way the flat table never could. The longer-tail outcome was the pattern itself entering the OneTrust design vocabulary as a reusable approach to hierarchical visualization. Subsequent projects with similar information shapes had a starting point.

Adoption of the map view by privacy customers was strong out of the gate — once compliance teams could finally see the chain of indirect vendors they were accountable for, the flat table stopped being a viable alternative. The bigger compounding outcome was downstream: the visualization pattern entered the broader OneTrust design system and was drawn from by multiple subsequent products that needed to show hierarchical relationships. The investment in pattern-level design IP paid back across the platform, not just inside Privacy.

RETROSPECTIVE

What I'd Do Differently

The timeline only allowed for internal testing before shipping, which I accepted at the time but would push back on now. With even two or three live customer sessions during the wireframe phase, we'd have caught a couple of edge cases around multi-persona support — legal vs. procurement vs. risk — that we ended up addressing in the next release rather than the first. I'd also have sequenced the design-system contribution alongside the product release instead of after; carving out a small, dedicated lane for pattern work during the build would have shipped the reusable IP a quarter earlier and saved every subsequent team the cost of relitigating the same visualization decisions.
