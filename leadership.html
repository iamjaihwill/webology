How I lead

Building teams, culture, and organizations that do exceptional work — with or without me in the room.

Great design leadership isn't measured by the work you make. It's measured by what keeps shipping after you've moved on.

Leadership Philosophy

People-Centered, Objective-Driven

I believe the best design teams are built on transparency, accountability, and genuine respect. When you treat the people around you as partners with ambitions as valid as your own, and actively help them succeed, you create the conditions for everyone to do their best work.

In practice, that means I hire people with a founder's mentality, give them clear objectives and the tools to succeed, then stay out of their way. I set high standards and hold the team (and myself) accountable. I don't tolerate ego-driven culture, but I do expect everyone to play at the top of their game.

This is the only way to build a culture of innovation that scales.

How I Build Teams

I hire for intellectual honesty, a bias toward action, and people who can hold strong opinions without being attached to being right. A polished portfolio matters, of course, but what I'm really paying attention to is how someone thinks when things get messy.

In interviews, I spend less time admiring outcomes and more time understanding decisions. I want to know where they struggled, what they missed, what they'd change, and whether they can talk about those moments without defensiveness. The people who grow the fastest are usually the ones who are honest enough to see themselves clearly.

One of the hires I'm most proud of was a designer who, on paper, probably looked slightly junior for the role. Their portfolio wasn't the flashiest in the stack, but there was depth in the way they thought through problems. During the interview, they walked me through a failed project with more clarity and self-awareness than most senior candidates talk about successful ones. I took the bet because I trusted their trajectory more than their résumé. Within two years, they had grown into one of the most dependable strategic designers on the team and eventually stepped into senior-level ownership. That experience reinforced something I've come to believe deeply: curiosity, humility, and self-awareness compound faster than polish.

I've also had hires that didn't work out. Early in my leadership career, I over-indexed on pedigree and independence for a role that actually needed collaboration and adaptability. The talent was there, but the working style created friction across the team and slowed momentum at a time when alignment mattered more than individual output. Since then, I've spent far more time evaluating how someone works with others, how they receive feedback, and how they navigate ambiguity — not just what they've shipped.

Across OneTrust, Fiserv, and AT&T, I've mentored more than five designers into senior roles — not by trying to manage their careers for them, but by helping them build clarity around where they were, where they wanted to go, and what was standing in the way.

What I've Learned the Hard Way

Early in one of my director roles, I inherited a team that had been operating in survival mode for a long time, and I moved too quickly trying to modernize everything at once.

I underestimated how long trust actually takes to build — especially with teams that have already experienced instability or constant change. The result was that some good ideas landed too aggressively, a few relationships became strained, and progress slowed because people didn't feel brought along with the change.

What I'd do differently now is spend the first stretch of any leadership role listening more intentionally than I speak. I map the informal influence structure early, understand where trust already exists, and identify which changes the organization is actually ready to absorb before trying to accelerate anything.

Design Leadership in the Age of AI

AI compresses the distance between idea and artifact. That's not a threat to design — it's a clarifying lens on what design actually is. When execution gets cheaper, the premium shifts almost entirely to judgment: knowing what to make, for whom, why it matters, and what it costs to get wrong. The designers who thrive in this environment will be the ones who use AI to think faster, not avoid thinking altogether.

At Paciolan, I'm helping shape how AI changes both our product strategy and our design practice. On the product side, we're exploring ways AI can reduce operational friction for clients, surface more intelligent recommendations, and create more adaptive experiences across the ticketing ecosystem. On the team side, I've introduced workflows that use AI to accelerate early exploration, sharpen product thinking, and create faster feedback loops without lowering the bar for craft or intentionality.

The skill I protect most fiercely is knowing which question to ask before reaching for a solution. AI is exceptional at producing answers. What it still lacks is discernment — the ability to understand which questions are actually worth asking in the first place. That remains deeply human work. And in many ways, that's the real work of design.
