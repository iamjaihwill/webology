Fiserv Commercial Banking Platform

Recovering a failed Phase 1 redesign and establishing the design-system foundation for a multi-channel banking platform

Role

UX Director

Company

Fiserv

Team

Internal UX team + offshore design partners

Domain

FinTech · Information Architecture · Payments

Key Outcomes

  • Recovered a failed Phase 1 redesign of a brandable multi-channel commercial banking platform — covering accounts, payments (ACH, Wires, Real-Time), and reporting — rebuilding stakeholder trust along the way
  • Made the strategic call to ground Phase 2 in real workflows and live user input rather than the visual-system-first approach that had failed in Phase 1 — phased improvements grounded in observed customer behavior
  • Structured the engagement across an internal UX team and offshore design partners — auditing the existing system, defining a scalable design architecture, and establishing reusable component patterns that scaled across product modules
  • Delivered a unified design system and modernized interaction patterns that became the foundation for long-term platform growth and consistent experience across every banking module — the highest-leverage outcome of the engagement

THE BUSINESS STAKES

Recovering a Redesign That Had Already Failed Once

Commercial banking platforms are the operating layer for how businesses move money. Accounts. ACH transfers. Wires. Real-Time payments. Reporting that gives a treasurer or controller the daily picture of cash. When the platform works, it disappears into the background of business operations. When it doesn't, it costs Fiserv's bank clients real customers — and costs Fiserv real revenue.

The platform had been through a Phase 1 overhaul before I was involved. Another team had led that effort, and it had failed with users. The redesign hadn't landed: stakeholders had lost confidence, customers were resisting the new flows, and the relationship between the design function and the business owners was strained. Phase 2 wasn't a greenfield engagement. It was a recovery.

KEY DECISIONS & TRADEOFFS

Real Workflows First, Visual System Second

The diagnosis was straightforward once we did the audit: Phase 1 had led with the visual system — a fresh skin and a new interaction language — without anchoring in how commercial banking users actually moved through their day. The new flows didn't match the workflows. The new components didn't match the patterns customers had built operational habits around. The redesign was technically newer and functionally worse.

I made the call to invert that order in Phase 2. The first work was workflow research — observing how cash managers, treasurers, payment-operators, and bank administrators actually used the platform. The design system would follow from that, not lead it. Phased improvements grounded in live user input, with each phase validated before the next began.

That reframing did two jobs at once. It produced a redesign that customers could adopt without breaking their operational habits. And it gave the business stakeholders a different narrative to take to their leadership: not "we're trying again," but "we're building forward from what we learned."

APPROACH

Audit, Architect, Standardize

The engagement structure mirrored the recovery framing. We audited the existing platform end-to-end — both the inherited Phase 1 work and the legacy patterns it had tried to replace — mapping every module (accounts, payments, reporting) to the user roles they served. Working with the internal UX team and offshore design partners, I directed the definition of a scalable design architecture: a system of reusable component patterns that any module could draw from, designed to scale across the platform's brandable multi-channel footprint without forcing every channel into identical surface treatment.

User-role mapping from the Phase 2 research: the bank's customer-support staff and the bank's customers (cash manager, payables, receivables, business administrator) — each with distinct workflows the platform had to serve.

WHAT SHIPPED

A Scalable, Brandable Enterprise Solution

Phase 2 delivered a unified design system across the platform's banking modules — consistent interaction patterns for the high-stakes workflows (initiating a wire, scheduling an ACH batch, reconciling a payment), and a brandable visual layer that let Fiserv's bank clients customize the experience without forking the underlying patterns.

The system was the deliverable that mattered most. It became the foundation for long-term platform growth: every subsequent module redesign drew from it, every new feature inherited the interaction patterns, and the cross-module consistency that had been missing in Phase 1 was finally a property of the platform rather than something individual designers had to recreate each time.

Outcomes

Created a foundation for long-term platform growth with consistent user experience across all banking modules. Stakeholder relationships rebuilt; design function repositioned with business and product partners. Specific adoption metrics and screens are NDA-sensitive and available in conversation for serious opportunities.

IMPACT & OUTCOMES

What the Recovery Made Possible

The most important outcome wasn't the redesign itself — it was the new operating model behind it. Phase 2 reset the relationship between design and the business: from "the team we tried before" to "the team that recovered the work." That repositioning is what unlocked subsequent platform investments and gave my UX team standing to lead, not follow, on later product decisions.

Adoption tracked ahead of the rollout plan, and the design-system patterns we established during the Phase 2 recovery were drawn from by subsequent commercial-banking modules — meaning the work didn't just ship a product, it shipped a foundation. The clearest indicator that the recovery had landed was a softer one: business stakeholders who had been skeptical at the start of Phase 2 began bringing us their next-quarter problems before they brought them to engineering. That shift in posture is what design recovery actually looks like.

RETROSPECTIVE

What I'd Do Differently

The inherited-Phase-1 audit was the right starting move, but I'd structure it as a shared exercise with the business stakeholders rather than a design-team deliverable. Walking through what hadn't worked with the people who had lived through it would have rebuilt trust faster and surfaced political context I had to learn the hard way over the following months. I'd also formalize the offshore-internal handoff cadence earlier — we eventually settled into a rhythm that worked, but the first six weeks lost time to handoff friction that a tighter weekly review structure would have absorbed. The single moment I'd revisit is the first stakeholder presentation: I led with the audit findings, which read as criticism of the Phase 1 team's work, when I should have led with what we'd protect from Phase 1 and built the criticism on top of that foundation.
