What design leadership owes its team right now

A few months ago, one of my senior designers told me something I didn't want to hear: "I don't know what I'm supposed to be getting better at anymore."

She wasn't complaining. She was reading the room more clearly than I was. The team had been using AI tools for several months. The output was faster, the iteration cycle was shorter, and the work was good. And under that surface, the things she'd spent her career sharpening — Figma fluency, component-system instincts, layout craft — were quietly becoming table stakes. The skills that were supposed to define her trajectory were no longer the skills she'd be judged by.

I'd been so focused on shipping the AI-touched product that I hadn't been doing the leadership work in front of me: helping my team make sense of what was changing, and what to invest in next.

This is the work I think design leadership owes its team right now, and it's harder than the strategy decks make it look. Three things I've been trying to do better.

1. Tell the team what's actually changing

The corporate version of a platform shift is sanitized in a way that doesn't help anyone. AI is augmenting our workflow. We're embracing new tools. This phrasing protects the company from looking flat-footed, but it leaves the team without a real read on what's happening to their craft.

The honest version is more useful and more uncomfortable. Some things are getting cheaper to produce — not just faster, cheaper — and that has career consequences for people whose value was wrapped up in producing them. The wireframe sprint that used to be a senior designer's signature contribution can now be a thirty-minute generation step. The component library port that used to take weeks of careful work can now be a pattern-match exercise. These aren't speculative changes. They're already true.

My team didn't need me to soften this. They needed me to name it. Once we'd named it, we could talk about what comes next without anyone feeling like they had to be quietly anxious about it on their own time.

2. Protect judgment time

When tools get faster, the natural managerial reflex is to expect more output. I caught myself doing this a few months in. The team was shipping more in less time, and the temptation was to fill the new capacity with more roadmap.

That's the wrong move, and I've come to think it's the move most design orgs are going to regret.

The thing AI compresses is execution time. The thing AI doesn't change is judgment time — the unglamorous, low-velocity work of figuring out what's actually worth building, what the user actually needs, what the business actually has to stand for. That work happens at the pace of conversation, research, disagreement, and reflection. You can't accelerate it by giving the team better tools, and you actively damage it by giving the team more roadmap.

The shift I've been making: protect judgment time as a first-class line item. Block discovery work, research synthesis, and design critique on the calendar the same way you block release windows. The team's compounding asset over the next five years isn't going to be how fast they execute. It's going to be how good their judgment gets. That requires deliberate practice, and deliberate practice requires the freedom not to be shipping every minute.

3. Be clear about which skills compound and which don't

This is the conversation I owe my team, and it's the hardest one.

Not all design skills are appreciating equally. Some are still core — and arguably more important than they were five years ago — because they're the work AI can't do. Others are starting to depreciate, fast, because they're exactly the work AI does well.

Compounding right now: research literacy, product instinct, systems thinking, the ability to facilitate strategic conversations, the ability to write clearly about decisions, comfort with ambiguity, an articulated point of view about taste. These are the skills where another year of practice makes you measurably better, and the work they unlock isn't replaceable.

Depreciating: pure tool fluency in any one platform, the ability to produce volume in isolation, encyclopedic knowledge of component states without judgment about which states matter, layout craft uncoupled from product reasoning. These aren't worthless — far from it — but they're no longer differentiating, and treating them as the developmental ladder is no longer fair to anyone climbing it.

The leadership work is being honest about this with each person on the team, individually, in the language of their actual career. Not "AI is changing everything." Not corporate happy-talk. A direct conversation about which of the things they're best at are going to keep paying back, and which they may want to layer something else on top of.

The trap I keep watching for

The trap is overcorrecting. There's a version of this conversation that scares people into thinking their current skills are obsolete, and that's both wrong and demoralizing. Most of what makes a senior designer valuable is still valuable.

The shift isn't from "your skills don't matter" to "you need entirely new skills." It's from "your skills make you fast" to "your skills make you trusted."

That's a meaningful difference. The designers who are leaning into that — who are getting better at the strategic conversation, the research design, the difficult-decision facilitation — are the ones whose careers I'd bet on for the next decade.

The other trap is undercorrecting. Telling the team nothing's changing, that we'll just keep doing what we've been doing with better tools, that AI is just a faster Figma. That version sounds reassuring in the moment and sets people up to be quietly shocked in two years when their next interview goes differently than the last one did.

What I'd tell a design leader watching this happen

If I were giving advice to a peer running a design org right now, it would be three sentences.

Name what's changing — out loud, with your team, before the discomfort becomes resentment. Protect the time it takes to develop judgment, because that's the skill that will define your team in five years. And have the individual career conversations now, in the language of compounding versus depreciating skills, while there's still time to invest before the market does the differentiation for you.

The platform shift isn't the thing to lead through. It's the thing that reveals whether you were leading at all.
